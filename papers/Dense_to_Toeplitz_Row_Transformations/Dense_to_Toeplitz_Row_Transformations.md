## Abstract

We consider transformations that receive a uniform dense matrix over $\mathbb Z_P$ and adaptively permute and negate its rows. For a uniform Toeplitz target of size $m\times n$, the probability of reaching the target support is at most $\min(1,2^{m-1}m!P^{-(m-1)(n-1)})$. The exact minimum total variation is determined by the source and target masses of signed-row orbits; it can be strictly larger than the support obstruction. If rejection on a single source draw is permitted, the largest acceptance probability producing an exactly uniform Toeplitz matrix is $P^{-(m-1)(n-1)}$. The zero orbit alone imposes this bound. We also give the complete acceptance-versus-error formula and an explicit small rectangular example separating support success from distributional accuracy. These results apply classical counting, total variation, and capacity arguments to a restricted matrix transformation class. Exact enumeration of 274,688 matrices and independent transport linear programs corroborate the formulas. The results concern matrix marginals and do not establish a general obstruction to cryptographic reductions.

## 1. Model and motivation

Fix $P=2^p$ with $p\ge2$, and $m,n\ge2$. Let $\Omega=\mathbb Z_P^{m\times n}$, and let $A$ be uniform on $\Omega$. A Toeplitz matrix has entries $T_{ij}=t_{i-j}$ for $0\le i<m$, $0\le j<n$. Write $\mathcal T$ for this set and $\nu$ for its uniform law. Its diagonals are free even over the composite ring $\mathbb Z_P$, so

$$
N=|\Omega|=P^{mn},\qquad t=|\mathcal T|=P^{m+n-1},\qquad
d=(m-1)(n-1),\qquad N/t=P^d.
\tag{1}
$$

Let $G$ be the group of signed permutations of the $m$ rows, of order $2^m m!$. A converter sees the entire matrix $A$, selects $U\in G$ using an arbitrary distribution depending on $A$, and outputs

$$
B=UA.
\tag{2}
$$

Thus the choice is adaptive, not independent of the source. There are no column operations, changes of dimensions or modulus, or rejection until rejection is explicitly introduced below. Optimization is information-theoretic: an optimal selection need not be efficient.

Two questions must be separated. Can the converter reach a Toeplitz matrix? Can it produce the uniform Toeplitz distribution? The latter requires correct masses inside the reachable support. Total variation, denoted $\Delta$, is half the $\ell^1$ distance and measures this distributional discrepancy [1].

### 1.1 A self-contained connection with rounding

Signed row permutations arise naturally when an affine operation must be computable from an already rounded vector for every input. The following proposition establishes this connection directly. The matrix theorems later in the paper only require the class (2).

**Scalar cell lemma.** Let $P=LQ$ with $L,Q\ge2$, and let $a,b\in\mathbb Z_P$. The values $[b+ar]_P$, $0\le r<Q$, all lie in one aligned cell $I_v=\{Qv,\ldots,Qv+Q-1\}$ if and only if $a=0$; $a=1$ and $b\equiv0\pmod Q$; or $a=-1$ and $b\equiv Q-1\pmod Q$.

**Proof.** The case $a=0$ is immediate. Otherwise, choose $1\le a<P$, put $g=\gcd(a,P)$, and let $\ell=P/g\ge2$ be its additive order. If $\ell\le Q$, the progression includes its entire coset of the subgroup generated by $a$. Its canonical representatives are

$$
u,u+g,\ldots,u+P-g,\qquad u=[b]_g.
$$

Their diameter is $P-g\ge P/2\ge Q$, whereas a cell has diameter $Q-1$. This is impossible. Thus $\ell>Q$, and the $Q$ distinct progression values occupy all $Q$ points of $I_v$. Translating by $a$ removes $b$ and adds $b+Qa$ modulo $P$. All $Q+1$ progression values are distinct, so $|I_v\cap(I_v+a)|=Q-1$.

Translation of the cell to $\{0,\ldots,Q-1\}$ gives, by counting the nonwrapped and wrapped parts separately,

$$
|I_v\cap(I_v+a)|=\max(0,Q-a)+\max(0,Q-(P-a)).
$$

Because $2Q\le P$, the two terms cannot both be positive. Their sum equals $Q-1>0$ only when $a=1$ or $a=P-1$. For $a=1$, the progression starts at the unique cell point whose predecessor is outside the cell, so $b=Qv$ modulo $P$. For $a=-1$, it starts at the unique cell point whose successor is outside, so $b=Qv+Q-1$. Both choices plainly suffice. In particular, for $Q=2$, the overlap must equal one, which gives the same two steps; a step of order two was already excluded by the diameter argument. $\square$

**Proposition 1.** Let $P=LQ$ with $L,Q\ge2$, and let $F(z)=\lfloor[z]_P/Q\rfloor$ act coordinatewise. If $U$ is square and invertible over $\mathbb Z_P$, then $F(Uz+b)$ is a function of $F(z)$ for every $z$ if and only if $U$ is a signed permutation and each positive row has $b_i\equiv0\pmod Q$, while each negative row has $b_i\equiv Q-1\pmod Q$. Without a translation, only ordinary permutations are possible.

**Proof.** Writing $z=Qy+r$ with $0\le r_j<Q$ gives

$$
F(Uz+b)=\left[Uy+\left\lfloor\frac{Ur+b}{Q}\right\rfloor\right]_L.
\tag{3}
$$

This identity uses arbitrary integer representatives of the coefficients. Changing a lift by $P$ changes the quotient correction by a multiple of $L$, which the outer reduction removes. It is necessary and sufficient that the remainder correction be constant on the zero cell.

Fix a row $i$. Set all remainders to zero except $r_j$, for a nonzero coefficient $U_{ij}$. The scalar cell lemma forces this coefficient to be $+1$ with $b_i\equiv0\pmod Q$, or $-1$ with $b_i\equiv Q-1\pmod Q$. A row cannot contain both signs: these two congruences on the same $b_i$ are incompatible since $Q\ge2$.

If a row contains two positive coefficients, choose their remainders equal to $Q-1$ and every other remainder zero. Relative to the zero vector, the quotient correction increases by $\lfloor2(Q-1)/Q\rfloor=1$. For two negative coefficients, write $b_i=Q\beta+Q-1$ and choose their integer representatives as $-1$. The same choice of remainders changes the correction from $\beta$ to $\beta-1$, because $-(Q-1)/Q$ lies strictly between $-1$ and zero. Neither change vanishes modulo $L\ge2$. These calculations include $Q=2$.

Thus each row has at most one nonzero coefficient. Invertibility excludes zero rows and repeated selected columns: with a repeated selected column, a square matrix of this form leaves another column zero. Hence $U$ is a signed permutation. Conversely, the scalar cell lemma and (3) show that every listed row gives an output determined by $y$. Finally, when $b=0$, the negative-row congruence is impossible for $Q\ge2$. $\square$

For a matrix-dependent choice, the universal premise must hold for every realized choice. Restricting it to a particular secret-dependent support would be a different condition. Furthermore, applying a permissible transformation to $Y=F(As+e)$ gives $F(UAs+Ue+b)$. A negative row's required translation may change the target noise law. Symmetry of the original noise does not remove that translation.

### 1.2 Matrix marginals and full observations

A converter receiving $(A,Y)$ may select $U$ using both inputs. Averaging its selection over $Y$ conditional on $A$ still gives a matrix kernel of the form (2). Therefore every marginal obstruction below remains necessary for such a converter. Attaining a marginal optimum does not establish that it is attainable together with a prescribed rounded output or secret correlation.

## 2. The support obstruction

Define $G\mathcal T=\{UT:U\in G,T\in\mathcal T\}$ and

$$
s_* =\frac{|G\mathcal T|}{N},\qquad h=2^{m-1}m!.
$$

**Theorem 1.** The largest probability of obtaining a Toeplitz matrix by (2) is $s_*$. Moreover,

$$
s_*\le\min(1,hP^{-d}),\qquad
\Delta(\mathcal L(B),\nu)\ge1-s_*\ge\max(0,1-hP^{-d}).
\tag{4}
$$

**Proof.** An input can be sent into $\mathcal T$ exactly when its orbit meets $\mathcal T$, or equivalently when it belongs to $G\mathcal T$. Choosing a witness on that set attains the claimed probability. This argument already allows the choice to depend on the whole input.

Global negation preserves $\mathcal T$, so $U\mathcal T=(-U)\mathcal T$. The group elements pair into $h$ pairs because $P\ge4$. The union therefore has at most $ht$ elements. Finally, the target is always Toeplitz, whereas the transformed source is Toeplitz with probability at most $s_*$. Testing this event gives (4). $\square$

Toeplitz membership is checked by comparing adjacent diagonal entries in $O(mn)$ operations. The obstruction therefore gives an efficient test even when orbit enumeration is expensive. The union bound need not be tight. For $P=4$ and $m=n=3$, it gives $s_*\le3/32$, improving the unpaired group count by a factor of two.

## 3. The exact distance from orbit masses

For an orbit $O$ of $G$ on $\Omega$, let

$$
p_O=|O|/N,\qquad q_O=|O\cap\mathcal T|/t.
$$

**Lemma 1 (fixed partition masses).** The achievable output laws are exactly the laws $r$ with $r(O)=p_O$ for every orbit. Their minimum distance from $\nu$ is

$$
\delta_* =\frac12\sum_O|p_O-q_O|.
\tag{5}
$$

**Proof.** No transformation moves mass outside its orbit. Conversely, any desired conditional law within an orbit can be sampled from every input in that orbit, since every destination is reachable by a group element. Projection onto the orbit index gives the lower bound in (5). To attain it, on an orbit with $q_O>0$ use the conditional target law with total mass $p_O$; on other orbits use any law of that mass. Each orbit contributes exactly $|p_O-q_O|$ to the $\ell^1$ distance. $\square$

Separating inaccessible orbits gives the useful identity

$$
\delta_* =1-s_*+\sum_{O:q_O>0}(p_O-q_O)_+,
\qquad (x)_+=\max(x,0).
\tag{6}
$$

Thus the support test misses excess source mass inside some accessible orbits.

**Proposition 2.** If $P^d\ge h$, then $\delta_*=1-s_*$.

**Proof.** Fix $T\in O\cap\mathcal T$ and let $H=\{I,-I\}$. Its $h$ cosets cover the orbit by translates of $HT$, so $|O|\le h|HT|\le h|O\cap\mathcal T|$. Since $N/t=P^d\ge h$, this gives $p_O\le q_O$. The extra term in (6) vanishes. $\square$

The condition is sufficient, not necessary.

## 4. Computing the signed-row orbits

Replace each row $r$ by the lexicographically smaller of $r$ and $-r$, and sort the resulting rows. This multiset is a complete invariant. If distinct row types have multiplicities $c_1,\ldots,c_j$, and $r_0$ counts all row occurrences whose type is not fixed by negation, then

$$
|O|=\frac{m!}{\prod_i c_i!}\,2^{r_0}.
\tag{7}
$$

The multinomial factor places the canonical row types into the $m$ labelled positions, accounting for repeated types through the factors $c_i!$. Once these positions are fixed, every occurrence of a type not fixed by negation has two distinct orientations, chosen independently at that position. This remains true for repeated types: the exponent counts occurrences, not distinct types. A type fixed by negation has only one orientation at each occurrence; its entries all belong to $\{0,P/2\}$. Every resulting matrix determines its row-type placement and orientations uniquely, so there is no additional overcount. Ignoring fixed rows or repeated types would incorrectly assume a free action.

Enumerating the $P^{m+n-1}$ diagonal sequences gives $|O\cap\mathcal T|$. Together with (7), this yields (4)–(6) without enumerating every dense matrix. The supplement also performs the larger dense enumeration as an independent finite check. Neither procedure is claimed to be polynomial in $m,n,\log P$.

## 5. Rejection on one source draw

We now allow the converter to return either a matrix in $GA$ or a rejection symbol. Let $\alpha>0$ be its acceptance probability, and require the accepted output to follow $\nu$ exactly.

**Theorem 2.** The maximum exact acceptance probability is

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{exact}}=P^{-d}.
\tag{8}
$$

**Proof.** The accepted masses must satisfy $\alpha q_O\le p_O$ for every orbit. The zero matrix is a singleton orbit with source mass $1/N$ and target mass $1/t$. Hence $\alpha/t\le1/N$, giving $\alpha\le P^{-d}$. Accepting precisely when $A\in\mathcal T$ and returning $A$ attains the bound. $\square$

The zero matrix is rare but cannot be discarded under an exact distributional requirement. Its argument uses only the implication $UA=0\Rightarrow A=0$, so the same exact acceptance upper bound holds if arbitrary invertible row operations are allowed. Repeating the identity filter on independent new draws takes $P^d$ draws on average. This statement presupposes access to those draws and does not describe a general adaptive multiple-instance reduction.

### 5.1 Acceptance versus approximation

Fix $0<\alpha\le1$ and permit an imperfect accepted output, including outputs outside $\mathcal T$.

**Lemma 2.** Its smallest conditional total variation is

$$
\delta(\alpha)=\sum_O\left(q_O-\frac{p_O}{\alpha}\right)_+.
\tag{9}
$$

**Proof.** If $r_O$ is the accepted mass in orbit $O$, then $0\le r_O\le p_O$ and $\sum_O r_O=\alpha$. The common mass with the target is at most $\sum_O\min(q_O,p_O/\alpha)$, proving the lower bound. For equality, first assign $r_O=\min(\alpha q_O,p_O)$. The remaining deficit is $D=\sum_O(\alpha q_O-p_O)_+$. Remaining capacities sum to $1-\alpha+D\ge D$ and lie in orbits already at their target mass or with zero target mass. Allocate the deficit there. Within each positive-target orbit use the conditional target law. Accepting from an input in $O$ with probability $r_O/p_O$ realizes this construction, with common mass equal to the stated bound. $\square$

At $\alpha=1$, (9) equals (5); it vanishes precisely for $\alpha\le P^{-d}$. This is a classical capacity optimization expressed in terms of the orbit constraint, rather than a new general rejection principle [2].

## 6. Exact finite examples

The following values come from complete enumeration of the stated domains.

| $P$ | Size $m\times n$ | Support success $s_*$ | Minimum distance $\delta_*$ | Exact acceptance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | $2\times2$ | $39/64$ | $25/64$ | $1/4$ |
| 4 | $2\times3$ | $211/1024$ | $813/1024$ | $1/16$ |
| 4 | $3\times2$ | $501/1024$ | $539/1024$ | $1/16$ |
| 4 | $3\times3$ | $4269/65536$ | $61267/65536$ | $1/256$ |
| 8 | $2\times2$ | $399/1024$ | $625/1024$ | $1/8$ |

For $P=4,m=3,n=2$, the support lower bound is $523/1024$, strictly below the optimum by $1/64$. Exactly four accessible orbits have excess source mass. Their canonical representatives, listed by rows, are

```
((0,1),(1,1),(1,2))    ((0,1),(1,2),(1,3))
((1,0),(1,1),(2,1))    ((1,0),(1,3),(2,1))
```

Each has 48 matrices, including exactly two Toeplitz matrices. Its source mass is $48/4096$, its target mass is $2/256$, and its excess is $1/256$. The four excesses give $1/64$. Transposing the dimensions changes the permitted operations, since only rows are transformed.

For this same domain, the rejection tradeoff is:

| Acceptance probability $\alpha$ | Minimum conditional distance |
|---|---|
| $1/16$ | $0$ |
| $1/8$ | $1/256$ |
| $1/4$ | $1/16$ |
| $1/2$ | $139/512$ |
| $1$ | $539/1024$ |

These are small-domain distributional calculations, not security estimates.

## 7. Boundaries of the transformation class

**Column changes matter.** Modulo $8$, take the rows of $A$ to be $(1,0)$ and $(2,3)$. No signed row permutation is Toeplitz: without exchanging rows one would need $1=\pm3$, and with an exchange one would need $2=\pm0$. Yet $A^2=I$, so allowing the column transformation $V=A$ gives the Toeplitz matrix $AV=I$. This refutes an extension of the support obstruction to arbitrary column operations. It does not produce a uniform Toeplitz matrix.

**A reflection needs its translation.** For $P=4,Q=2$, the values $0$ and $1$ have the same quotient but their negatives do not. The corrected reflection $x\mapsto-x+1$ satisfies $F(-x+1)=[-F(x)]_2$.

**Toeplitz and Hankel have the same orbit saturation.** The matrix with rows $(0,1)$ and $(2,0)$ is Toeplitz but not Hankel, so the supports are different. Their conversion problems under (2), however, are equivalent. Let $\mathcal H$ be the Hankel support and let $R\in G$ reverse the row order. If $T_{ij}=t_{i-j}$, then

$$
(RT)_{ij}=t_{m-1-(i+j)}.
$$

This depends only on $i+j$, and the inverse row reversal sends every Hankel matrix to a Toeplitz matrix. Thus $R\mathcal T=\mathcal H$. Since $R^2=I$ and $R$ acts within each $G$-orbit,

$$
G\mathcal H=G\mathcal T,\qquad
|O\cap\mathcal H|=|O\cap\mathcal T|\quad\text{for every orbit }O.
$$

The two target sets also have equal size. Consequently, their uniform target masses $q_O$ agree orbit by orbit. They have the same optimal support success $s_*$, minimum distance $\delta_*$, exact acceptance threshold, and entire curve $\delta(\alpha)$ in this matrix conversion model. Column reversal also exchanges the supports but lies outside (2); row reversal already supplies the equivalence using an allowed operation.

For a source $(A,F(As+e))$ and a target whose public matrix is always Toeplitz, the error of the full view is at least the error of its matrix marginal. Our bounds are therefore necessary whenever the output matrix remains in $GA$. Conversely, an optimal matrix kernel need not preserve the observation or its secret dependence. Conditioning solely on $A\in\mathcal T$ preserves independent secret and noise laws, but it incurs the acceptance cost in (8).

## 8. Verification and relation to prior work

The exact verifier uses integers and `fractions.Fraction` to examine **274,688 dense matrices** in the five displayed domains. It compares canonical orbit counting with a direct search for a signed ordering whose row prefixes and suffixes match. The latter does not use the canonical representative to decide reachability. Every enumerated orbit size is checked against (7), and complete target-orbit profiles and tradeoff values are supplied.

A separate transport program builds all reachable destinations directly at matrix level, without importing the orbit implementation. It solves six linear programs: three acceptance rates each for $(P,m,n)=(4,2,2)$ and $(4,3,2)$. SciPy/HiGHS results are compared with the exact fractions at tolerance $10^{-8}$. Floating-point optimization corroborates but does not certify the general formulas. Negative controls implement the three examples in Section 7. An additional standard-library check directly tests the scalar cell lemma, affine rows and small invertible matrices, and checks the Toeplitz/Hankel target-orbit correspondence and the orbit-size formula by explicit group images. Its exact domains and counters are recorded in `data/structure_results.json`; finite checks corroborate the proofs rather than extending their hypotheses.

The passage to partition masses and common-mass maximization are standard total-variation tools [1]. Budgeted rejection for general divergences is studied by Verine et al. [2]; (9) is the elementary finite capacity form needed here. Block and Polyanskiy [3] instead study selection among several independent draws, with bounds in terms of the number of samples. Their broader sampling model is not excluded by our one-draw conditional argument.

The contribution of this note is the explicit diagnosis of signed-row conversion to the Toeplitz distribution: the paired support count, exact orbit formulas, the rectangular excess-mass example, and the zero-orbit acceptance threshold. It does not establish impossibility for transformations that change columns, secrets, moduli, dimensions, supports, or the number of instances. No hardness reduction or security claim follows from these matrix calculations alone.
